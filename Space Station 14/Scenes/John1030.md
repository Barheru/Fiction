Haymitch gets home from his shift in different clothes than he had left in. 

He's not fully sure how far this change in him goes. His God has turned out to wear two faces, and both are that of death: the bloated, drowned face of the river, and the burning, smoke-choked place where a face should be. While Moira was right about many things, she was wrong when she had said that the “angel” was not of God. 

No, both the Death without and the Death within him is of the Lord—Haymitch is sure of that. Their affection for him is as he was always taught his god's was: jealous; personal; disinterested; callous; sadistic; doting, condescending. He glances down at his left hand, where the skin of his ring finger is puckered and warped like it had been dipped into hot oil, and healed with the skin still puckered and warped by that deep burn.

He’d always asked why his god had not saved him from the “gift” that Cydonia had given him. The others who had witnessed that flame had cried out to the divine and been saved; he'd often wondered why he hadn't been. He’d often feared that his god, like Cydonia’s, had abandoned him.

He knows, now, that he was never abandoned. *Jacob have I loved, and Esau have I hated.* Haymitch has most certainly been loved by his god. He was never abandoned; when he cried out for God to save him, his god had responded: *“Which of you, if your son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake? Or if he asks you to save you, will you take your fire from him?”*

His god has drawn very near indeed.

Declan finds him there, sitting on the couch, wrestling with God like his namesake, chewing on his sleeve. Haymitch feels his father’s mind approach before he hears his footsteps. Although he does not fully remember the week of his fear and the fight that he and his father and his god had waged in his head, through it, Declan’s mind has become to him as familiar as his own. He knows the patterns of Declan’s thoughts and emotions like he knew the cadence of Erik’s footsteps as a child.

He feels Declan reach out to him with his own mind, like he's knocking on his bedroom door, and he opens it without hesitation. 

Declan’s sadness flows into his mind like a wind blowing through that open door between them. Haymitch knows that there's something else in Declan's mind, too: anxiety. He can't feel that anymore, though. His god has taken that as burnt offering. 

Declan sits down on the couch next to him, and Haymitch moves to lean against him. Declan puts his arm around his son’s shoulders.

*“I saw it again today.”* His father’s words are so, so hesitant. Haymitch can feel the underlying layer of Declan’s reticence to scare or upset him. 

*“No, you didn't.”* Haymitch slips his right cybernetic hand into Declan’s. *“I thought I saw it, too—but it was the Angel of Death wearing its face.”*

He opens those memories to Declan. It is more efficient than trying to tell all that has happened today with words. He simply lets Declan move through the memories through Haymitch's own eyes, watching him speak with the angel wearing Aisling’s face. Declan feels the memory of its gentle touch, sackcloth on sackcloth. He sees Moira's rage and grief, and the way stars had filled her face and her love had finally broken through the cracks that Liath and Cydonia had put in the shell of his resistance to letting anyone love the dying man he was. He lets his father watch him burn the sackcloth. 

Declan is silently crying by the time he has finished Haymitch’s recollections. Haymitch wants to pull back from his father’s pain; he wants to close that door. Declan’s grief is almost overwhelming. It is deeper than he has almost ever let himself feel. He did not weep like this when he let Itzial drift; he did not weep like this for Kam and Tidus; he did not weep like this in the depths of his pain. He has never let himself.

Declan, however, weeps for his son. 

*“Moira is a smart woman.”* Declan’s words are accompanied by a small ray of affection and hope, and Haymitch realizes that it is by Declan—and not Cydonia—that Moira called him her brother. Declan loves them both as his children; he catches a fragment of a memory of Moira’s voice calling him “dad.” *“And she is wise in this. We would never let you die alone.”* Declan’s hands tremble. 

*“I don't want to die.”* A memory stirs, fragmented by pain and the burned ends of his neurons. He'd said those words to Tidus, maybe, that one, brief time he had let his former friend hold him, skin on fur, in the incense-thick darkness of the chaplain's bedroom on station. 

He realizes that Declan has caught the brush of that memory, too—but it doesn't matter. His father watched him die over and over in his mind this week. Those deaths were something that a parent should never see—an intimate moment is a much lighter violation compared to the burned body of your child. 

Declan had no answer for him. They both know there isn't one—at least not an easy one. Moira may have ideas, of Cydonia, or even Listh, but those are for tomorrow. Tonight is for—

*“For family.”* 

They both shiver in perfect unison.

Declan frowns slightly, pulling back both physically and mentally from Haymitch. 

“Did you—” Haymitch flushes. He's not quite sure how to describe what he'd felt for that brief instant. But for a single moment, he had been both himself and Declan—not simply registering his father’s thoughts and emotions, but holding them equally as his. 

Declan does not reply. Instead, Haymitch feels his father once again reach out that gentle psychic hand to that door between them.

Haymitch considers not opening that door. Declan’s grief, even tasted briefly, was an overwhelming flood that he could drown in if he let it sweep him away. And that deep, wounded part of him—that part that Moira had called the rot in him, like a lightning-struck tree—screams that Declan will only hurt all the more when he loses Haymitch if Haymitch lets him closer. 

But Moira had said she would not miss being at his deathbed, and Liath had said she wanted to hold him with whatever time they had left, and Cydonia had already prayed he had died surrounded by those he loved, and he is finally ready to believe them all. 

He opens the door between his own mind and Declan’s once more, pulling his legs up onto the couch to curl against his father. 

*“So you felt it too, then?”* Haymitch asks.

*“Like two lenses shifting into focus with each other?”* Declan puts his arm around Haymitch’s shoulders again. *“Yes.”*

Haymitch is silent for a moment, letting his breathing match his father’s. He could fall asleep here. The weight of his emotional exhaustion makes it easier to let Declan’s awareness sink deeper into his own. 

*“We'll probably feel all of it. See all of it. Do we want that?”* They both shiver again. They notice that the rhythm of their breathing is actually perfectly aligned now. They suspect the beating of their hearts is, as well. 

*“We want that.”*

The dull headache that Haymitch’s body carries like a millstone around his neck is felt by both bodies, now. The joints of both ache slightly, as Declan’s do. Their fatigue divided itself equally between their bodies—or rather, their body. It is one in two parts, only as separate as the right hand is from the left. 

They smile, in spite of the fact that there is technically more pain now. Each part of the body is used to its own pain. It is only the combination that is new to them. 

They sit like that for a long moment, letting themselves rest in the physical wholeness, before their focus turns inward. There is a weight of memories now that is new—and in that weight, potential. It reminds them of the thrill of unread books on a shelf. They cannot possibly take it all in, but there is so much intoxicating potential. 

*“We do not touch some memories,”* they warn themselves. *“If it is warm to the touch, we block that thought. We are good at this. We have always been good at this.”* It is a tricky dance. But it is also as Haymitch had told Moira: in order to not look at the elephant in the room, you look at something else. If both of them had not been trained in this, they would have failed; but they were both trained. The control they have, even over a shared mind, is exactingly precise. 

*“This reminds me of when we moved in together.”* They both smile. The movement is, again, synchronous. *“There is so much to unpack. I am excited to unpack it, but it is too large of a task to do all at once.”*

There is one memory, of course, that they do not wait to share:

Cydonia. 

*“I wish we had all of our memories. But this is so much more than we had.”* As if from a box of photographs, they produce the image of it, with its dark feathers and dark eyes. The memory is not perfect. None of their memories from Haymitch’s mind are; they are burned at the edges. They cannot recall if it was shorter then than it is now. They cannot recall if its head feathers were shorter. But they hold that memory as precious nonetheless, sinking into kt&—into the memory of soft feathers and tears inside a circle of white paint. 

*“We need to make sure it knows how much we love it.”* Their bodies shift slightly against one another, relaxing further into the couch as the last vestige of doubt leaves them. *“It is the only reason I have this peace.”*

This peace is not one that will be easily shaken; in this moment, they doubt it can ever be.
